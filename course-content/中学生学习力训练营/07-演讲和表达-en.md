# 07 · Presentation & Expression → Speech Draft + Feedback

## Learning Goals
1. Use AI as a "speech coach": help organize arguments, design structure, polish expression — not write the script for you.
2. Finish a speech draft of your own and build a "self-assessment + peer-assessment" feedback loop.
3. Improve logical organization and spoken-expression awareness (opening — body — ending).
4. Hold the red line: the core content of the script comes from you; AI only gives structure and expression advice.

---

## Warm-up Question
> Your class needs a 3-minute speech "AI in My Eyes." What will you do?
> A. Let AI write the whole thing and memorize it. → Not your voice; the teacher hears it instantly, and you can't deliver it alive.
> B. First list 3 points you want to make, then ask AI to check "is the structure clear, which part needs an example," and write the script yourself.
>
> A good speech has "your idea" first, then "good expression."

---

## Core Concepts

### 1. The three-part speech structure
- **Opening**: grab attention (a story / a question / a contrast).
- **Body**: 2–3 arguments, each with 1 example or data point.
- **Ending**: echo the opening, leave one memorable point.

### 2. AI's correct role
- **Structure advisor**: check if your outline logic flows.
- **Example coach**: suggest "add a life example here to make it livelier."
- **Expression polisher**: point out where it's too formal, where there are too many filler words.
- **Not a ghostwriter**: it doesn't decide "what you want to say."

### 3. Two sources of feedback
- **Self-assessment**: record yourself / read once, find stumbles and weak spots.
- **Peer-assessment**: classmates give feedback by a standard (content / structure / expression).

### 4. Spoken vs. written
Speeches use short sentences and natural connectors; avoid long, hard sentences and piled-up jargon.

## Real Learning-scenario Cases

### Case: Topic "Why study history"
- **Wrong**: "Write me a speech on the meaning of studying history." → all AI, no you.
- **Right**: You first list "1. know where we come from 2. learn methods 3. avoid being misled" as three points, then ask AI: "Is my structure smooth? Can you give a direction for a historical anecdote I can fill in for part two?" You write the script, then ask "is my opening catchy enough?"

### Case: English speech "My Hero"
- **Wrong**: AI writes the whole English script; you read it but don't understand.
- **Right**: You write Chinese key points; AI helps turn key sentences into natural English and explains why; you finally deliver in your own words.

## Right vs. Wrong Use

| | ❌ Wrong | ✅ Right |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Script source | AI writes it all | You write it; AI gives structure advice |
| What AI gives | A complete new script | Structure / example / expression feedback |
| What you do | Memorize AI's script | Express yourself, revise yourself |
| Improvement | None | Logic + expression both improve |

## Hands-on Task
1. Pick a 3-minute speech topic; first list your own "opening — 3 arguments — ending" outline.
2. Give the outline to AI; ask it to give feedback only on "is the structure clear, which part lacks an example," not to write the script.
3. Write the speech yourself (300–400 Chinese characters / 150–200 English words).
4. Record yourself reading it once; use the "self-assessment form" to find 2 improvable spots; then ask a classmate for "peer-assessment form" feedback.

---

## Prompt Template (student version)
```
I'm a [grade: ______] student doing a [3]-minute speech on [topic: ______].
Here is the outline I wrote myself: [paste: ______].
Please do NOT write the script for me. Please:
1. Evaluate whether my structure is clear (opening / body / ending);
2. Point out which part most needs an example, and give me a "fill-it-yourself" example direction;
3. Check if my expression is too formal, and give 1 spoken-language suggestion.
I will write the script myself.
```
> Red line: your viewpoint, your script; AI is only a coach, it doesn't speak.

## Common Mistakes
1. **Ghostwritten speech**: all-AI script, loses personal voice.
2. **Take-but-don't-revise**: collect advice but don't act on it, script doesn't improve.
3. **No practice**: finished but never read or recorded, freezes on stage.
4. **Ignore peer feedback**: only self-assessing leaves blind spots; peer review fills them.

## Lesson Summary & Next Steps
A good speech has "your idea" first, then "good expression." AI fits the role of structure advisor and expression coach, but the soul of the script must come from you. The "self + peer" dual feedback improves faster than practicing alone — others immediately see where you stumble, go empty, or drift off topic.

### Five tips to beat stage nerves
1. **Three deep breaths at the opening**: steady the first sentence and the rest flows.
2. **Lock onto "friendly faces"**: find a nodding, encouraging classmate and speak to them.
3. **Use "cue words" not the full text**: remember only keywords, improvise naturally.
4. **Allow pauses**: a pause isn't dead air, it lets the audience digest.
5. **Use AI as a rehearsal audience**: deliver to AI first, let it point out what it doesn't understand, fix ahead.

### A note for parents/teachers
Giving children a real audience and real feedback matters more than a perfect script. At home, do a weekly "3-minute share" on any topic; at school, use the peer form for classmate feedback. Expression is trained, not talked about.

### Quick self-check card (30 seconds before going on stage)
- Are the outline and core viewpoint mine? Yes → continue.
- Is the opening—body—ending structure complete? Yes → continue.
- Have I practiced at least once and revised? Revised → speak with confidence.
With a "share" mindset rather than a "perform" mindset, nerves drop by more than half.
