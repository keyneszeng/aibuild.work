# 06 · Judging True from False → Fact-Checking Practice

## Learning Goals
1. Understand AI "hallucination": it confidently makes up wrong facts, fake references, and fake data.
2. Master a basic judgment framework for "fact vs. opinion" and "source credibility."
3. Build a practical fact-checking checklist — verify every factual claim AI gives you before using it.
4. Apply the red line "check every fact first" throughout all learning activities.

---

## Warm-up Question
> AI tells you: "Einstein failed math in elementary school and only became great later." Do you believe it?
> In fact, this is a widely spread **urban legend** — Einstein was always good at math.
> AI won't tell you it's false on its own — **it only knows "this gets said a lot," so it says it.**
> This lesson is dedicated to practicing "how not to be fooled by AI."

---

## Core Concepts

### 1. What is "Hallucination"
A large language model generates text by predicting "the next plausible word," not by "looking up the truth" in a database. When it doesn't know, it "makes up something that sounds reasonable" — that's a hallucination. It **never thinks it's wrong**.

### 2. Three types of information most likely to be wrong
- **Specific facts**: years, names, data, places (AI often mixes them up).
- **Citations / references**: it often fabricates "looks-real" book titles, authors, and papers.
- **Latest information**: its training data has a cutoff date and knows nothing after it.

### 3. Fact vs. Opinion
- **Fact**: verifiable as right or wrong (e.g., "water boils at 100°C").
- **Opinion**: a subjective judgment (e.g., "this movie is great").
- For "facts" from AI, verify them; for "opinions," check whether they have a basis.

### 4. Source-credibility ladder
Primary sources (textbook / official document) > authoritative media / academic database > ordinary webpage > social media / forum > screenshots from unknown origin.

## Real Learning-scenario Cases

### Case: Historical date
- AI says "the Xinhai Revolution happened in 1912."
- Check: the textbook says 1911 (the Wuchang Uprising). AI confused "Republic founded in 1912" with "Xinhai Revolution in 1911."
- Action: verify with a textbook / authoritative encyclopedia and mark the correct year.

### Case: Scientific data
- AI says "the speed of light is 30,000 km/s."
- Check: it's actually about 300,000 km/s — a 10x error.
- Action: for any data, check an authoritative source before citing it.

### Case: Reference citation
- AI gives you a passage "a paper proves…" with an author and journal name attached.
- Check: search the academic database — no such paper exists. A classic fabricated citation.
- Action: never cite a reference AI gives you unless you have verified the original yourself.

## Right vs. Wrong Use

| | ❌ Wrong | ✅ Right |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Getting a fact | Believe it and put it in homework | Mark it "to be checked" |
| Verifying | None | Cross-check with authoritative sources |
| Citing | Copy what AI gave | Cite only after finding the original |
| Attitude | "AI is always right" | Doubt facts first, believe after checking |

## Hands-on Task
1. Ask AI 3 factual questions (e.g., "the year of an event," "a scientist's contribution," "a data point").
2. For each answer, verify it with a textbook / authoritative website; record "What AI said / What's actually true / Was it wrong?"
3. Find at least 1 AI mistake and write down how you discovered and verified it.
4. Fill in your "Fact-Checking Checklist" (below).

---

## Prompt Template (student version)
```
I'm working on [subject/homework: ______] and need to verify a fact: [fact statement: ______].
Please:
1. First tell me whether this is a "fact" or an "opinion";
2. If you're unsure or the info may be outdated, tell me clearly "I cannot guarantee accuracy";
3. Give me 1–2 directions for authoritative sources I can check myself (e.g., a textbook chapter, an official site);
4. Do not fabricate references.
```
> Red line: for any factual information, verify before use; never cite an unchecked AI reference.

## Human Review Checklist (self-check)
- [ ] I marked the factual info AI gave me as "to be checked."
- [ ] I cross-checked with at least 1 authoritative source.
- [ ] I did not cite any reference I hadn't verified myself.
- [ ] I can give 1 example of an AI hallucination.

---

## Common Mistakes
1. **Accepting everything**: putting AI's "facts" straight into homework / exams.
2. **Citing fake references**: using book titles/papers AI made up — academic misconduct.
3. **No cross-check**: relying only on AI's single voice.
4. **Confusing fact and opinion**: treating AI's subjective judgment as objective truth.

## Lesson Summary & Next Steps
AI "confidently talks nonsense" — that's its most dangerous trap. Facing any factual information, put a question mark first, then cross-check with authoritative sources. That is the most basic information literacy of the AI age. Remember: **what's credible is not "what AI said" but "what reliable sources confirmed."** Check every fact first — write this into your user-manual red line.

### Four types of high-mimicry rumors (be alert when you see them)
1. **Misattribution**: pinning A's achievement onto B (e.g., crediting someone else's discovery to a celebrity).
2. **Precise fabrication**: giving a very specific fake number or year to look authoritative.
3. **Invented references**: fabricating book titles, authors, journals — nothing found on search.
4. **Outdated info**: answering today's question with old data, without noting the time limit.
For all four, the first reaction should be: verify with an authoritative source.

### A note for parents/teachers
Play a "spot-the-error game" with your child: deliberately give an AI answer mixed with mistakes and see if the child can catch and verify them. Turning "seeing through false info" into a point of pride works better than repeating "don't trust the internet."
