# Lesson 2 — Differences from Traditional Products

## I. Learning Goals

- **Knowledge**: Understand the essential differences between AI products and traditional software — probabilistic output, the need for evaluation, uncertainty, dependence on data and feedback; master the basic perspective of "AI opportunity analysis."
- **Ability**: Identify whether a requirement is "suitable for AI"; use a framework to judge whether AI delivers value that traditional methods cannot.
- **Artifact**: An *AI Product Opportunity Analysis* (including a fit judgment + a list of differentiators).

> This lesson references the Discover/Define stages of the deanpeters framework, Generative-AI-For-Beginners' discussion of "when to use AI," and mlabonne/llm-course's notes on LLM boundaries.

## II. Warm-up Question

> Add an "AI summary" to the search box and that's an AI product?
> Why can some features be done with rules, yet insist on using a model?
> The model answers wrong once and your product's trust collapses — how do you design for that?

Without understanding the difference, you'll "do AI for AI's sake" and build something expensive and fragile.

## III. Core Concepts

### 1. Probabilistic vs Deterministic
Traditional code: fixed input → fixed output. AI: the same input may give different outputs, and can be wrong. The product must be "fault-tolerant + interruptible."

### 2. Evaluation Is a Must
Traditional features test "right or wrong"; AI features must test "good or bad" — relevance, safety, experience — requiring a test set and metrics (see Lesson 7).

### 3. Data Flywheel
AI products improve through usage data (feedback loop); traditional products don't necessarily. This is AI's moat, and also its source of risk.

### 4. Human–Machine Collaboration Boundary
What to let AI do, and what must be decided by a human? Draw clear "human-in-the-loop points" (see Lesson 5, Agent).

## IV. Worked Example (Real Product Scenario)

### Case: Customer-Service Bot
- Traditional: keyword matching with fixed replies — rigid but controllable.
- AI: understands intent and answers flexibly, but can make things up. By design it must "answer based on the knowledge base + escalate to a human when unsure."
**Difference**: flexibility ↑ but needs evaluation / fallback; can't blindly replace the old one.

## V. Hands-on Task

- **Background**: You have a feature you want to build.
- **Input**: feature description.
- **Steps**:
  1. Use the "fit-judgment template" to assess whether AI should be used.
  2. List 3 differentiators from the traditional approach.
  3. Mark the mandatory human-in-the-loop points.
- **Expected output**: *AI Product Opportunity Analysis*.

## VI. Prompt / Design Template

```
You are an AI product advisor. I want to build [feature].
Please help me judge: ① whether AI is necessary for this need (vs. rules / traditional approach) ② the unique value AI brings ③ the biggest risk ④ the mandatory human-in-the-loop points.
Give a conclusion using [suitable / not suitable / proceed with caution] with reasoning.
```

## VII. Human Review Checklist

- [ ] Explicitly states the AI-necessity judgment
- [ ] Differentiators are specific, not boilerplate
- [ ] Human-in-the-loop points are identified

## VIII. Common Mistakes

1. Wanting to "add AI" to every feature.
2. Ignoring the cost of evaluation and fallback.
3. No human-in-the-loop points designed — when something goes wrong, blame the model entirely.

## IX. Homework (Deliverables)

Submit the *AI Product Opportunity Analysis*, including the fit conclusion + differentiators + intervention points.

## X. Grading Rubric

- Completion: analysis complete (40%)
- Quality: judgment is evidence-based, differentiators are real (35%)
- Risk: intervention points are clear (25%)
